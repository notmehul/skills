# Words and Expressions Commonly Misused

From *The Elements of Style*, Chapter IV. Many of these are not so much bad English as bad style — the commonplaces of careless writing. The proper correction is often not replacing one word with another but replacing vague generality with definite statement.

## A

**Aggravate / Irritate.** *Aggravate* means "to add to" an already troublesome condition. *Irritate* means "to vex" or "to annoy."

**All right.** Two words — *all right*. Never *alright*.

**Allude / Elude.** You *allude* to a book (indirect mention); you *elude* a pursuer. *Allude* is not synonymous with *refer* (which is direct).

**Allusion / Illusion.** *Allusion* = indirect reference. *Illusion* = unreal image or false impression.

**Alternate / Alternative.** *Alternate* = every other one in a series. *Alternative* = one of two (or more) possibilities.

**Among / Between.** *Among* for more than two considered as a group. *Between* when each is considered individually, even if more than two: "an agreement between the six heirs."

**And/or.** Damages sentences and leads to confusion. Avoid.

**Anticipate.** Use *expect* for simple expectation. *Anticipate* implies acting in advance of.

**Anybody / Anyone.** One word in the sense of "any person." *Any body* = any corpse/group. *Any one* = any single person/thing.

**As good or better than.** Rearrange: "My opinion is as good as his, or better" (not "as good or better than his").

**As to whether.** *Whether* is sufficient.

**As yet.** *Yet* usually suffices.

## B

**Being.** Not appropriate after *regard...as*: "He is regarded as the best dancer" (not "regarded as being the best dancer").

**But.** Unnecessary after *doubt* and *help*: "I have no doubt that" (not "no doubt but that"). "He could not help seeing" (not "could not help but see").

## C

**Can.** Means "am able." Not a substitute for *may* (permission).

**Care less.** "I couldn't care less" — not "I could care less."

**Case.** Often unnecessary: "In many cases, the rooms lacked air conditioning" → "Many of the rooms lacked air conditioning."

**Character.** Often redundant: "acts of a hostile character" → "hostile acts."

**Claim (verb).** With object-noun, means "lay claim to." Not a substitute for *declare*, *maintain*, or *charge*.

**Compare.** *Compare to* = point out resemblances between different orders (life compared to a pilgrimage). *Compare with* = point out differences between same orders (Congress compared with Parliament).

**Comprise.** Means "embrace." A zoo *comprises* mammals, reptiles, and birds. Animals do not *comprise* a zoo — they *constitute* it.

**Consider.** Not followed by *as* when it means "believe to be": "I consider him competent" (not "consider him as competent").

**Contact (verb).** Vague and self-important. Get in touch with, phone, find, or meet people instead.

**Currently.** Usually redundant with a present-tense verb.

## D

**Data.** Plural. "These data are misleading" (not "the data is").

**Different than.** Use *different from*. One thing differs *from* another.

**Disinterested / Uninterested.** *Disinterested* = impartial. *Uninterested* = not interested. Not interchangeable.

**Due to.** Loosely used for *because of* in adverbial phrases. "He lost because of carelessness" (not "due to carelessness").

## E

**Each and every one.** Pitchman's jargon. Avoid.

**Effect / Affect.** *Effect* (noun) = result; (verb) = to bring about. *Affect* (verb) = to influence.

**Enormity.** Means "monstrous wickedness." Do not use for bigness.

**Enthuse.** Not recommended. Use *enthusiastic* or *talked enthusiastically*.

**Etc.** Least objectionable when a list is nearly complete. A misfit in formal writing.

## F

**Fact.** Use only for matters of direct verification, not judgment.

**Facility.** Why say "facility" when you mean jail, hospital, or school?

**Factor.** Hackneyed. Usually replaceable with something more direct.

**Farther / Further.** *Farther* for distance. *Further* for time or degree.

**Feature.** Hackneyed. Usually adds nothing.

**Finalize.** Pompous and ambiguous. Avoid.

**Fix.** Colloquial for *arrange*, *prepare*, *mend*. Prefer its standard meanings: "to make firm," "to place definitely."

**Flammable / Inflammable.** The standard English word meaning "combustible" is *inflammable*. *Flammable* is a modern coinage used on warning signs because some people misread the *in-* prefix as a negation. Unless you are labeling a vehicle or container, use *inflammable*.

**Folk.** Collective noun, equivalent to *people*. Use singular. *Folks* for "family" is colloquial.

**Fortuitous.** Means "happening by chance." Not synonymous with *fortunate* or *lucky*.

## G

**Get.** *Have got* for *have* should not appear in writing. Preferred past participle: *got*, not *gotten*.

**He is a man who.** Redundant: "He is a man who is very ambitious" → "He is very ambitious."

**Hopefully.** Once meant "with hope." Now distorted to mean "I hope." Avoid this usage — it is both wrong and silly. Rephrase.

**However.** Avoid starting a sentence with *however* when meaning "nevertheless." It works better when not in first position.

## I

**Imply / Infer.** *Imply* = suggest or indicate (speaker/writer does this). *Infer* = deduce from evidence (listener/reader does this).

**Importantly.** Rephrase: "More importantly, he paid" → "What's more, he paid."

**In the last analysis.** A bankrupt expression.

**Insightful.** A suspicious overstatement for "perceptive."

**Interesting.** An unconvincing word. Don't announce that what follows is interesting — make it so.

**Irregardless.** Not a word. Use *regardless*.

**-ize.** Do not coin verbs with this suffix. *Utilize* → *use*. *Finalize* → *finish/complete*.

## K-L

**Kind of.** Not a substitute for *rather* or *something like* in formal writing.

**Lay / Lie.** *Lay* is transitive (lay/laid/laid): the hen lays an egg. *Lie* is intransitive (lie/lay/lain): the llama lies down.

**Leave / Let.** "Let it stand" (not "Leave it stand").

**Less / Fewer.** *Less* for quantity. *Fewer* for number. "Fewer workers" (not "less workers").

**Like.** Not a conjunction. Use *as* before phrases and clauses: "as in the old days" (not "like in the old days").

**Literal / Literally.** Do not use to support exaggeration: "a literal flood of abuse" → "a flood of abuse."

**Loan.** A noun. As a verb, prefer *lend*.

## M-N

**Meaningful.** A bankrupt adjective. Choose another or rephrase.

**Most.** Not a substitute for *almost*: "almost everybody" (not "most everybody").

**Nature.** Often redundant, like *character*: "hostile nature" → "hostile."

**Nauseous / Nauseated.** *Nauseous* = sickening to contemplate. *Nauseated* = sick at the stomach. Say "I feel nauseated" unless you mean you sicken others.

**Nice.** Shaggy and all-purpose. Best reserved for "precise" or "delicate": "a nice distinction."

**Nor.** Often wrongly used for *or* after negatives: "He cannot eat or sleep" (not "eat nor sleep"). Use *nor* with *neither*.

**Noun used as verb.** Suspect. Prefer *give* to *gift*, *host* → *give/hold*, *debuted* → *made its debut*.

## O-P

**One.** Followed by *one's*, not *his/her*: "One must watch one's step."

**-oriented.** Clumsy and pretentious. Find a better way.

**Partially / Partly.** *Partially* = to a certain degree (condition). *Partly* = a part as distinct from the whole. Usually *partly* is what you want.

**People.** Best not used with numbers. "Six persons" (not "six people").

**Personalize.** Pretentious. Avoid.

**Personally.** Often unnecessary: "Personally, I thought it was good" → "I thought it good."

**Possess.** Often used to sound impressive when *have* or *own* will do.

**Presently.** Ambiguous (means both "soon" and "now"). Best restricted to "in a short while."

## R-S

**Regretful / Regrettable.** *Regretful* = feeling regret. *Regrettable* = causing regret. Don't confuse them.

**Relate.** Not intransitive for rapport: "I relate well to Janet" → "Janet and I have much in common."

**Respective / Respectively.** Can usually be omitted.

**Shall / Will.** In formal writing: *shall* for first person, *will* for second and third. *I shall* = belief about the future. *I will* = determination.

**So.** Avoid as an intensifier: "so good," "so warm."

**Split infinitive.** Avoid unless placing unusual stress on the adverb.

**State.** Not a substitute for *say* or *remark*. Restrict to "express fully or clearly."

**Student body.** Needless and awkward. Just say *students*.

## T

**Than.** Check that no essential words are missing: "I'm closer to my mother than to my father" (not "than my father" — ambiguous).

**That / Which.** *That* = restrictive (defines): "The mower that is broken is in the garage." *Which* = nonrestrictive (adds): "The mower, which is broken, is in the garage." Go which-hunting.

**They.** Do not use with singular distributive antecedents (*each*, *everybody*, *everyone*). Strategies: use plural subjects, eliminate the pronoun, or switch to second person.

**Tortuous / Torturous.** *Tortuous* = winding. *Torturous* = painful.

**Transpire.** Means "become known," not "happen."

**Try.** Takes the infinitive: "try to mend" (not "try and mend").

**Type.** Not a synonym for *kind of*: "that kind of employee" (not "that type employee").

## U-W

**Unique.** Means "without like or equal." No degrees: not "most unique" or "very unique."

**Utilize.** Prefer *use*.

**Verbal / Oral.** *Verbal* = pertaining to words. *Oral* = pertaining to speech. *Oral agreement* is more precise than *verbal agreement*.

**Very.** Use sparingly. Where emphasis is needed, use words strong in themselves.

**While.** Use only in the literal sense of "during the time that." Not a substitute for *and*, *but*, or *although*.

**-wise.** Don't use as a pseudosuffix (*taxwise*, *pricewise*). Fine when it means "in the manner of" (*clockwise*).

**Worth while.** Overworked as vague approval. Use a stronger word: *promising*, *useful*, *valuable*, *exciting*.

**Would.** When habit is already expressed (*once a year*, *every day*), the past tense without *would* is usually better: "Once a year he visited" (not "would visit").
