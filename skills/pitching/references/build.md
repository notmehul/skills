# Building the pitch

Phase scripts, the tension-loop library and the hot-cognition stack for branch 1 of `pitching`. Ask one question per slot, then draft; the pitch should read as a narrative with minute markers.

## Phase 1: you and the big idea (minutes 0 to 6)

**Set the time frame (0 to 1).** Constrain your own time first. It signals confidence and removes the listener's fear of being trapped. Script: "I've only got about 20 minutes to give you the big idea, which leaves us time to talk it over before I have to get out of here."

**Track record (1 to 3).** Under 2 minutes. People form an impression from the average of what they hear, not the sum, so one great accomplishment beats one great plus two mediocre. Ask for the single most impressive thing built, shipped or done that bears on this pitch, and write 3 or 4 sentences with only that in it. No life story.

**Why now (3 to 5).** The brain detects motion. Two static states ("the old way, the new way") do not register; three forces pushing the idea forward do. Ask for each and weave them into momentum:

- Economic: what financial shift creates the opening (rates, funding, cost changes, market growth).
- Social: what behaviour or cultural change supports it.
- Technology: what new capability or cost drop makes it possible now.

**The idea introduction pattern (5 to 6).** Fill the brackets, then smooth it into spoken language:

> For [target customers]
> who are dissatisfied with [current offerings],
> my idea is a [new category]
> that provides [the key solution].
> Unlike [the competing product],
> my idea is [the key difference].

## Phase 2: budget, secret sauce, competition (minutes 6 to 15)

The longest phase and where pitches lose the room. The antidote is tension loops: push, then pull, two or three times, woven into the narrative rather than listed.

- **Budget**: show skill at budgeting, which is respected, rather than optimistic revenue projections, which are discounted.
- **Competition**: two questions only. How easy is it for new competitors to enter? How easy is it for customers to switch?
- **Secret sauce**: the unfair advantage in one or two sentences. When the pitch is for a tool, run it live on their own artefact (their repo, their server, their data) instead of a sample. Proof on their artefact beats any claim.

### Tension-loop library

Each loop has a push (take something away), a pause of 2 or 3 seconds, and a pull (offer it back, conditionally).

| Intensity | When | Push | Pull |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Low | First meetings, cautious rooms, early in the pitch | "There's a real possibility we might not be right for each other." | "But then again, if this did work out, our forces could combine into something great." |
| Medium | Warm leads, mid-pitch when engagement is good | "What really counts is having someone in charge who has passion, experience and integrity. If you and I don't share that view, it would never work between us." | "But that's crazy to think. Obviously you value people over smart ideas." |
| High | Disengagement, drift, last resort | "Based on your reactions, it seems like this isn't a good fit. Let's wrap this up and agree to get together on the next one." Start packing up. Be willing to leave. | The pull comes from them. They let you leave (the deal was dead) or they re-engage ("Wait, let's keep going"). |

To build a custom loop: name something the listener values about this deal, threaten to remove it, pause, reintroduce it with "but if...".

### Novelty injections

The brain stops attending to anything it thinks it already understands. Mid-pitch, add an unexpected metaphor, a pattern interrupt (tone, pace, position), a counterintuitive fact about the market, a prop that breaks expectations, or a question that reframes the conversation.

## Phase 3: the deal (minutes 15 to 17)

Clear and brief, no drill-downs: what will be delivered, when, how, their role and responsibilities, and the one risk they are carrying with the gate that removes it (a paid 30-day pilot with a named milestone and a clean exit if it is missed). Remind the user that the most important deliverable in the deal is them. People invest in people.

## Phase 4: the hot-cognition stack (minutes 17 to 20)

People decide they want something before they understand it; brain scans put the decision up to 7 seconds before conscious awareness, and the data gets collected afterwards to justify it. Stack four frames in quick succession so cold analysis shuts down and wanting takes over.

1. **Intrigue.** Deliver the pre-prepared story (the "man in the jungle" pattern: a difficult situation, beasts attack, will he reach safety) and stop before the resolution. Go back to the pitch. Finish the story after the business is done. Ask: what real situation involved risk, time pressure and an uncertain outcome related to this work?
2. **Prize.** One or two lines: "I'm choosy about who I work with." "Can you tell me why we would enjoy working with you?"
3. **Time.** A real, fair constraint: "This deal will be fully committed by [date]." Good deals are like a train: they stop at the station, pick up passengers and leave on schedule. Name the constraint behind the date first (a round close, a cohort start, a capacity limit); if the listener could not check it or you would not hold to it, leave the time frame out. An invented deadline costs more than none. No extreme pressure; it reads as cheap.
4. **Moral authority.** The strongest frame. "We do things right. No games, no missing numbers, just clean paper." Then ask them to confirm shared values: "Can you play by the rules?"

## Post-pitch (after minute 20)

The most dangerous moment; small mistakes here get amplified into deal killers. Instead of "So, what do you think?", make three points:

1. This deal will be fully committed in [timeframe], only with a real constraint behind it.
2. We don't need your [money, partnership], but we want the strategic value you bring.
3. I think you're interesting, but are you really the right fit? We need to know more about you.

Then stop talking and wait. Before leaving, set one next step on your terms with a date: "I have Thursday at 2 for the pilot scope." Withdrawal without a date is how deals go quiet.

## What the build hands back

1. The complete script by phase with minute markers.
2. Two or three tension loops written as dialogue.
3. The intrigue story, cut at the right moment.
4. The four-frame stack, scripted.
5. The post-pitch script with the dated next step.
6. A one-glance timing card for the room.
