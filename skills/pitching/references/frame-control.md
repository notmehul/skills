# Handling the room

Frame tactics, status moves and the scenario playbook for branch 3 of `pitching`. Every recommendation ends in words the user can say out loud.

## The five laws of frames

1. Everyone uses frames whether they realise it or not.
2. Every encounter brings different frames together.
3. Frames do not coexist; they collide.
4. Only one survives. The stronger frame absorbs the weaker.
5. The winning frame governs the interaction.

You hold the frame when they are reacting to you. If you have to explain your authority, power or advantage, you do not. Frames are won by behaviour, never by argument.

## Frame move or real constraint

Sort this before anything else. A committee, a security review, a budget cycle or a legal requirement is a real constraint: answer it straight, with evidence, and move on. Frame tactics are for status plays, and used on a real constraint they read as evasion.

## The three opposing frames

### Power frame

Signals: arrogance, disinterest, rudeness, making you wait, interrupting, checking the phone, changing the agenda unilaterally.

Counter with the power-busting frame. Observe no power rituals (no fawning, no deference, no small talk that exists to establish their rank). Use small acts of denial and defiance with humour. They reach for your materials: pull them back, "Not yet. You have to wait for this." They say "I only have 15 minutes": "That's okay, I only have 12." Smile, and mean it. Defiance without humour is hostile; with humour it creates local star power. Once you take the frame, move the meeting in your direction at once.

### Time frame

Signals: attention fading, bored body language, watches checked, side conversations, "let's wrap this up".

Counter with the time-constraining frame. Nobody else sets your clock. When attention is dying, end it first: "Looks like time is up. I've got to wrap this and get to my next meeting." Never talk faster to cram more in; running long is neediness. Break an incoming time frame with a prize frame: "No, I don't work like that. I need to know: are you good to work with? Can you keep appointments?"

### Analyst frame

Signals: line-by-line drill-downs, spreadsheet demands, "what are the numbers", more data requested before any decision.

Counter with the intrigue frame. Hot cognition (desire) and cold cognition (analysis) cannot run at the same time. Give one high-level answer and redirect to the relationship: "Revenue is $80M, expenses $62M, net $18M. You can verify that later. Right now: are we a good fit? Should we be doing business together?" Then the intrigue story.

## The prize frame

Works against all three. You are the prize; the money, the opportunity, the partnership needs you. Money is a commodity, available in a thousand places; there is one of you.

Basics: make them qualify to you ("Tell me more about yourself. I'm picky about who I work with"); protect your status (they do not change the agenda, the time or the attendees); if the decision maker does not show, "I can wait 15 minutes, then I have to leave", and leave.

Advanced: no trial closes ("So, are we in the ballpark?"); issue challenges ("So many buyers, yet only one of me. How are you going to compete for my attention?"); make them perform a legitimate task to earn the deal. Openers: "I'm glad I could find the time to meet with you today." "Remind me again why in the world I want to do business with you?"

## The intrigue story

Prepare one and reuse it on every audience; every croc brain responds to the same elements. It must be brief and relevant, have you at the centre, carry risk and uncertainty, a ticking clock, a force blocking you, and serious consequences if you fail. The pattern: put a man in the jungle, have beasts attack him, hold him at the edge of safety. Tell only part of it and go straight back to the business. The unfinished story spikes dopamine and breaks the analyst frame; finish it after the deal is done.

To draft one, ask: what real situation had risk and an uncertain outcome? What was the clock? What blocked you? What were the stakes? Then write it and cut it before the resolution.

## Status

Global status (wealth, position, fame) is fixed. Situational status is fluid, changes with the environment, and can be seized, especially by moving the conversation into a domain where you are in charge. It resets every time you leave and re-enter an encounter.

Beta traps, each a ritual that reinforces someone else's status: the lobby (sign in, take a seat, visitor badge), the conference room (wait for the important people), public spaces (coffee shops, trade-show booths), small talk, being late, accepting schedule changes, deferring to their expertise on everything.

To seize situational status: ignore the power rituals politely and avoid the traps; be unaffected by their global status; find small denials and defiances; move the discussion into your domain the moment you have the frame; apply the prize frame; confirm it by making them qualify ("Remind me again why I should want to do business with you?"). If you start from beta, be on time; lateness gives power away. Create status at once; hesitation loses it. Have fun; nothing is as attractive as someone enjoying their work.

## Neediness after the case is made

Never: "Do you still think it's a good deal?", "So, what do you think?", "We can sign right away if you want us to." Instead, three points, then silence:

1. This will be fully committed in [timeframe], only with a real constraint behind it.
2. We don't need [what they offer], but we want the strategic value you bring.
3. I think you're interesting, but are you really the right fit? We need to know more about you.

## Scenario playbook

**They make you wait.** Lobby, badge, 15 minutes, a conference room, 10 more minutes, the investor arrives and checks their phone. A beta-trap sequence plus a power frame. Stand near the entrance rather than sitting; walk with the assistant, not behind. Take a different chair from the guest seat and move one small thing. When they arrive: stand, eye contact, "Good to see you. I've got another meeting across town, so let's get right into this. I'll give you the big idea in about 15 minutes." If they check the phone mid-pitch, pause in silence; when they look up, smile: "You want me to wait?" Then continue.

**The CFO demands unit economics line by line at minute 8.** Analyst frame. "Gross margins are 68%, improving to 74% by Q3 as we scale. The full model is in the deck I'll leave with you. But the numbers only matter if we're the right partners. Let me tell you what happened when we launched the pilot..." If they push again: "I appreciate the rigour; it tells me you're serious. I'll walk you through the complete model in a follow-up. Today I want to make sure we're aligned on vision."

**"We're talking to three other vendors."** Commoditisation. "That makes sense. We're also evaluating whether this is the right engagement for us. Not every partnership is worth our time. What are you actually looking for? Because if it's the cheapest option, we're probably not your people."

**The meeting gets cut short.** Time frame. "No problem. I was going to suggest we wrap up anyway. Let me leave you with the one thing that matters..." Deliver the single strongest point. "I'll send the details. The next step depends on whether you're serious about this. Are you?" Stand first and pack up.

**They renegotiate after agreement.** Power frame plus a neediness test. Three seconds of silence. "Interesting. What changed since our last conversation?" Listen. "We agreed those terms because they reflect the value we bring. If the terms change, the scope changes. Want me to walk you through what that looks like?" If they push: "I want this to work, but I'm not starting a partnership by undermining the agreement we already made. That's not how we operate. Are we still aligned on values here?"

**Introduced as the junior person.** Status assignment. Do not correct the introduction. First statement, demonstrate domain expertise: "I led the [result] that brought us to this conversation." Then a question that puts you in control: "[Senior person], how are you thinking about [hard topic in your domain]?" Move to your expertise fast; general social territory makes the reset harder.

**Silence after the proposal.** A week, no reply. The instinct to chase is neediness. Send no "just checking in". Send one thing of value unrelated to the deal (an introduction, market intel). One follow-up at most, about your timeline: "We're moving forward with commitments for Q2. If this isn't the right time, no worries at all. Let me know either way by Friday so I can plan." "No worries at all" plus a real deadline is the anti-neediness sweet spot.

## Principles for any scenario

1. Identify the frame before reacting.
2. Never respond from need. If you feel desperate, wait; it leaks through every word.
3. Humour disarms. A well-timed joke in a frame collision signals confidence.
4. Movement signals ownership: standing, the whiteboard, a change of seat.
5. Silence is a tool. After a strong statement, stop talking.
6. Always have an exit. If you cannot credibly leave, you cannot credibly negotiate.
